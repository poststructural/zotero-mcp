# Strand revision checklist (from Dorothea's 11.16 feedback on Canon/Canon Wars)

Source: Dorothea's email of Nov 16, 2026 and her 46 margin comments in `da_comments_11.16_Canon_CanonWars.v5.docx`. Comment numbers (C#) refer to that file. This checklist turns her section-specific comments into rules that transfer to the Censorship, Moral Panics, and Text Selection strands. It is a working tool, not an accepted project decision.

Use it alongside `rubric.md` (dimensions in brackets), `dorothea_standards.md`, `slop_patterns.yaml`, and `critical-synthesis-new.md`.

## A. Section architecture

1. **Open each section by saying what kinds of sources it reviews and in what order.** If a section moves from commentaries to studies, say so in the first sentence (C131). Make clear the studies concern teachers' text selection in the period the strand covers (C2). [2, 9]
2. **Open each paragraph with what it is about and what it shows**, and how it follows from the previous one. No paragraph should open on a detail (C135). Add a transition where the move is unannounced (C139). [2, 3]
3. **Follow the order you set up.** If the intro names X then Y, discuss X first (C19). [2]
4. **Subheadings are a road map.** They name the major points of the argument and are grammatically parallel within a section. If one subheading says "Survey studies of…", its sibling says "Qualitative studies of…" (C222, email item 6). [2, 3]
5. **The conclusion reflects the body's main points**, and only those. Revise it last (C234). [3, 6]

## B. Genre and attribution

6. **Label every source as commentary or research study** at first mention (C138, C140). [9]
7. **Name who conducted a survey or analysis**; no agentless "surveys found" or "analysis showed" (C16, C186, C187, C188). [7, 9]
8. **Name the publication** when a commentary's venue matters (C149). [9]
9. **Give people agency.** Books do not replace books; teachers, committees, or departments do (C150). [1, 7]
10. **Do not disguise political stances.** When public or academic actors take positions, identify their stance (conservative, progressive, etc.), separate opposing figures rather than listing them together, and represent the other side of the debate (C60, C68, C72, C110). [7]
11. **Only report motives the source documents.** "How do you know he was reluctant?" (C137). Don't attribute aims a study did not set out to pursue (C232). [6, 7]

## C. Survey studies (email items 1–2)

12. **State what the survey asked**: which texts were assigned, required, or available; who chose them and why; how much autonomy teachers had. Say who responded (teachers, chairs, administrators) (email item 1, C197). [5]
13. **Integrate all of a study's measures.** If one survey reports both what was taught and teachers' reported autonomy, present them together and say how they relate (C190). [5, 2]
14. **Keep the reader oriented to which study and which year** is under discussion across consecutive paragraphs (e.g., 1989 vs. 1992 reports) (C13, C190). [2]
15. **Simplify the data dicing.** Introduce grade bands, lists, and denominators once, in plain terms, and stay with one frame where possible (C168, C171, C182, email item 2). Give the denominator and say whether a number is large ("out of how many? Is this a lot?" C58). Consider a short table if the comparisons pile up (C196). [1, 6]
16. **Interpret change sensibly.** Note the time span before claiming continuity or change ("it's only 4 years," C184). [6]
17. **Spell out abbreviations and explain terms** at first use (C17, C59, C67, C144, C197). [1, 4]

## D. Qualitative studies (email items 3–4)

18. **Use Dorothea's study shell** (C228) for each qualitative study:
    1. Design and setting: "In her case study of a suburban high school near Portland, Oregon…"
    2. Focus: what the researcher examined (e.g., efforts to revise a required American literature course).
    3. Data and duration: observation and interview data collected over seven weeks.
    4. What happened, concretely: who chose what text, against what alternatives, for what stated reasons.
    5. What the study documents about teachers' text selection.
    6. The mechanism or condition it reveals (e.g., notions of appropriateness and suitability that circumscribe selection at the department level).
19. **Be concrete about observations and objects**: "observations of what?", "replacement of what — the major text? the course?", "what did they challenge about the text?" (C226, C227, C230). [1]
20. **Drop the repeated generalizability caveat.** State the insight the study gives that surveys cannot (e.g., selection driven by sociopolitical commitments and personal preferences) (C231, email item 4). A single note on scope for the group of studies is enough, if any. [6, 12]
21. **Delete loose qualifiers** like "traditional" when they are not defined (C223). [4]

## E. Tie to the strand's framing argument (email item 5)

22. **Say why these studies are here.** Each strand's study review should state explicitly how the studies relate to the strand's historical or political frame (for Canon: the 1980s–90s canon wars). Dorothea's reading of the Canon argument: continuity persists in the face of the wars. Each strand needs an equivalent thesis, stated early and returned to. [3, 8]
23. **Check claims against findings.** "The findings you report don't suggest this" (C207). Clarify vague referents like "these" (C220). Make central-versus-decentered claims explicit (C105). [6]

## F. Final screen

24. Rubric pass on dimensions 1–9 and 12; slop screen; varied openings; citation rule (paraphrase: author-year only; quotation: verified locator); `[CHECK]` on anything unverified.

## G. Cross-strand architecture (PROPOSED, not yet accepted by Austin)

Goal: the four strands read as one review, with the same skeleton, so Dorothea's road-map rule (item 4) holds across the chapter as well as within each section.

**Common skeleton for each strand**
1. *Strand introduction*: the strand's question, the order of sources, and the key findings stated up front.
2. *Historical or political frame*, in chronological order (Canon: formation and the university canon wars; Moral Panics: the episodes from Americanization to current campaigns; Censorship: challenges, bans, and policy).
3. *Research on secondary English teachers' text selection under that frame*, divided by method with parallel headings, e.g. "Survey Studies of …" / "Qualitative Studies of …".
4. *Summary of key findings*, labeled, followed by a transition to the next strand.

**Heading style across strands**
- Content headings name the point or period, not a question ("What Library Research Can Establish about Rejection" becomes a noun phrase).
- Sibling headings share a grammatical form. Avoid mixing colon titles ("Save Our Children: Casting Teachers as a Threat") with plain noun phrases at the same level unless every sibling uses a colon.
- Use stable terms across strands: "text selection" for choosing texts; "challenge," "ban," "rejection," and "restriction" defined once in Censorship and used the same way in the other strands.

**Current headings (10.20.26 drafts), for comparison**
- Censorship: How Challenges Change Teachers' Selections; Banning and Rejection in Contemporary Policy; Rejecting a Book Before Anyone Challenges It; What Library Research Can Establish about Rejection; Rejection and the Authority to Select.
- Moral Panics: Moral Panic, Curricular Restriction, and Text Selection; Political Contention and Curricular Authority; Teaching Loyalty: Americanization and the Red Scares; Jim Crow, Desegregation, and Teachers' Curricular Authority; Teaching War and National Loyalty: Vietnam and September 11; Save Our Children: Casting Teachers as a Threat; Secular Humanism and the Textbook Wars; Moral Panic and Contemporary Curriculum Campaigns; Teachers' Text Selections under Political Pressure.
