# Dorothea's standards (draft v1)

Built from 206 of Dorothea Anagnostopoulos's margin comments on 8 of Austin's drafts (Nov 2025 to Oct 2026), plus her feedback emails of Jun 24, Jul 1 and Oct 28, 2026. Comments were hand-coded by Claude; Austin has not reviewed the codes yet. Counts are comments touching each theme (one comment can carry several).

## What she asks for, most to least often

| # | Standard | Comments | Her words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Say it concretely.** Replace abstraction with the plain claim, an example, a number, or the participants' words. | 42 | "Your writing is still at a more abstract level that makes it difficult to understand what exactly is going on." / "What does this mean?" |
| 2 | **Make the order visible.** Chronology first; announce moves between kinds of studies with a transition. | 37 | "I would make this part of the review more chronological." / "You need a transitional sentence or two here to note that you are moving to different types of studies." |
| 3 | **Use precise, stable terms.** Pick one term, keep it, and flag analytic uses ("grammar of schooling") against everyday ones. | 33 | "Use of 'field' is confusing here again." / "Keep your language specific to text selection. Curriculum is larger than that." |
| 4 | **Anchor every study.** Research question, method, sample, specific findings, before any interpretation. | 29 | "Again, more specific, concrete details about method and findings are needed." / "You have the methods down but not the questions." |
| 5 | **Only claim what the studies show.** Check accuracy; don't overstate across studies. | 28 | "Do the studies show that?" / "Is that the case across all of the studies or just these two?" |
| 6 | **Serve the section's question.** Intro material and side arguments go elsewhere; remember it is a literature review. | 16 | "Stay focused on what this tells us about teachers' text selection." / "Loosing the thread that this is a lit review." |
| 7 | **Sort by genre and level.** Study vs. essay; university vs. secondary; state inclusion criteria; justify non-US studies. | 13 | "Is this a research study or is it more of an essay? What were your inclusion/exclusion criteria?" |
| 8 | **Mark whose claim it is.** The author's conclusion or yours; their term in quotation marks. | 12 | "Is this Rios' conclusions or yours? That has to be made clear in a review of the lit." |
| 9 | **Identify people and texts.** A descriptor for every name; a clear grammatical subject. | 11 | "Whose this? Provide a descriptor." / "Did he design this unit? Make the subject clear here." |
| 10 | **Signpost key findings, repeatedly.** Label the summary; state findings more than once in drafting. | 9 | "It might be better, at this point, to hammer your key findings several times." |

Less frequent: her suggested theoretical links (governmentality, cultural memory, 7), cautions about using your own classroom as data (5, TKM proof only).

## A tension to settle in the rubric

Her push toward the concrete and declarative ("keep the focus on text as an actual book here rather than text more as discourse") can pull against poststructural moves in your `academic-writing` skill. Her own comments suggest a resolution: concrete in the review of studies, theoretical once the empirical ground is laid, which is the sequence principle in your `critical-synthesis` skill.
