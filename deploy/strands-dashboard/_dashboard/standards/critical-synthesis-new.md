
# Critical Synthesis — The Granular Layer

## Overview and Purpose

This skill governs the *granular layer* of Austin's critical synthesis — the body-paragraph moves that establish empirical and methodological ground for individual studies before the theoretical argument can land. It is not a replacement for `/academic-writing`; it is the prerequisite for it.

Dorothea's recurring critique names a sequencing failure: critical argument arrives before the reader knows what the studies actually did or found. The solution is not to add more theory — it is to slow down at the level of the evidence itself. A reader cannot follow the claim that "inherited curriculum constrains teacher selection" if they haven't yet seen, in specific empirical terms, what studies documented about that inheritance, in what samples, using what methods.

**The governing sequence is:**

1. **Ground** — Name the studies. Convey methods, sample, setting, and key finding.
2. **Synthesize** — Show what the studies, taken together, demonstrate. Name convergences, divergences, and gaps.
3. **Theorize** — Only then does the theoretical argument arrive as the explanation demanded by the evidence.

This skill governs steps 1 and 2. `/academic-writing` governs step 3.

## Make the Order Visible

Dorothea's most frequent recent comments: "This is a confusing paragraph - what you say is later is earlier. I don't understand the chronology here." / "In this section first fully discuss Applebee and then the earlier and then later studies and how they confirm or extend Applebee's findings." / "Before you talk about how it went to the larger public, provide information on the canon wars within academia."

**Rules:**
1. **Pick an order and keep it.** Chronological by default for historical material. If you depart from chronology, say why.
2. **Foundational study first, in full.** Then earlier studies, then later ones, each framed by how it confirms or extends the foundational one. Say "Applebee's (1989) survey updated his 1974 history" rather than leaving the reader to infer the relationship.
3. **Context before consequence.** Describe a debate where it happened (the university canon wars) before tracing how it reached secondary classrooms.
4. **Announce moves between kinds of studies.** "You need a transitional sentence or two here to note that you are moving to different types of studies." Use plain transitions that name the move ("Survey research documents what was taught; a second line of qualitative studies asks how teachers chose"), never motif callbacks.
5. **Open each section with a roadmap that names concrete content.** Dorothea praised this opening ("Much better!"):

> Before reviewing studies of how and why teachers selected texts during this period, I first trace the formation of an American secondary canon as it was shaped by college entrance requirements and the professional reports that revised them. I then turn, briefly, to the canon wars in American universities... Finally, I turn to studies that explicitly examine secondary English text selection, focusing first on contemporary commentaries and then moving to research studies. These studies show that required readings in secondary schools changed slowly and largely at the margins...

She flagged a structurally similar roadmap whose terms were vague ("the selection apparatus they have inherited," "three movements"): "This term is too vague." The roadmap is welcome; abstraction inside it is not.

## Signpost Key Findings

"It might be better, at this point, to hammer your key findings several times." State the key findings in the section opening, return to them as each study confirms or complicates one, and **label the summary paragraph** ("Summary" as a heading, or "In sum, these studies show three things:"). On a summary she liked, she asked Austin to "label it Summary," to name each force, and to show how they relate before moving on.

## Identify People, Texts, and Genres

- **A descriptor for every name:** "Whose this? Provide a descriptor." → "Donelson (1985), a former president of NCTE, ..." The same for commentaries: "By who? Teachers, teacher educators, university English faculty?"
- **A clear grammatical subject:** "Did he design this unit? Make the subject clear here."
- **Sort by genre and level:** say whether a source is a research study, a practitioner essay, or a policy report, and whether it concerns universities or secondary schools. State inclusion criteria; justify non-U.S. studies.
- **Book titles in the text** when a book consolidates several studies ("His book, *Literature in the Secondary School* (1993), consolidated four studies...").

## Stable Terms

Pick one term per concept and keep it. Dorothea flagged "field" (confusing), "curriculum" (larger than text selection), and "required" ("is this the right word?"). Mark analytic terms ("grammar of schooling") against everyday ones, and keep the focus on texts as actual books in review sections rather than text as discourse.

## The Sequence Principle

Empirical grounding must precede critical synthesis. This is an epistemic requirement, not a conventional one. The theoretical argument earns its authority from the specificity of what it explains. If the reader doesn't know that Zancanella (1991) studied eleven teachers in a single suburban district — not a national sample, not a random draw — then the claim about the situated, contextual nature of text selection floats without evidence.

Within any synthesis section:

**Do this in order:**
1. Establish the empirical terrain — what studies exist, who conducted them, on whom, where, through what methods
2. Name convergences and divergences across studies
3. Identify gaps in the record — what the studies couldn't or didn't examine
4. Build toward the synthetic claim — what this body of evidence, taken together, permits
5. Pivot to theory — what framework makes the pattern legible

**Common violation:** Invoking the theoretical claim in step 1 or 2, before steps 1–4 have been executed. The result is an argument that sounds compelling but lacks the evidential infrastructure to persuade a skeptical reader.

## Study Anchoring: The Core Granular Move

The most important move in this skill. When naming a specific study, embed its essential descriptive information — authors, year, sample size, participant type, geographic context, study design — *in the attribution itself*, not in a separate clause or methods parenthetical.

**The pattern:**
> `[Author(s)] ([year]) + [brief study descriptor with method/sample embedded] + [specific finding]`

**Examples from Gilmore (2025):**
> "In Murray and Male's (2005) seminal study of 28 teacher educators in England, more than a third of the participants had no clear sense of how research and teaching might be interrelated and symbiotic as activities in academic life."

> "In a year-long professional development program for 11 teacher educators in Israel, Shagrir (2010) found that combining theoretical reading, practice-based discussion, and peer feedback enabled participants to articulate the uniqueness of the teacher educator profession."

> "In an international study of 41 teacher educators in Ireland, Israel, the Netherlands, and Norway, Guberman et al. (2021) reported that around 75% had prior experience teaching in schools."

**What to embed in the attribution:**
- Sample size: "28 teacher educators," "a cohort of 16 doctoral students," "eleven secondary English teachers"
- Participant type: "novice teacher educators," "PE faculty in their first two years," "secondary English teachers in a single district"
- Geographic context: "in England," "across four institutions in the U.S.," "in a single midwestern suburban district"
- Study design, when it matters: "in a longitudinal self-study," "through a survey of 346 teachers," "via semi-structured interviews across three sites"
- Temporal scope, when relevant: "during their first year in the role," "over a two-year follow-up period"

**What NOT to do:**
- "Several studies have examined this phenomenon (Murray & Male, 2005; Guberman et al., 2021; Griffiths et al., 2010)." — This claims convergence without demonstrating it. Parenthetical clusters are not evidence.
- "Murray and Male (2005) investigated teacher educator identity development." — This names the study but conveys nothing about what it found or how.
- "Research shows that teacher educators struggle with the research-teaching divide (Murray & Male, 2005)." — Invisible methods, invisible findings, invisible argument.

## The Specificity Sandwich

The structural pattern for presenting study-level evidence within a thematic argument. It ensures that granular evidence is always framed by the synthetic claim it supports.

**Structure:**
> [Thematic claim] → [Named study with method + specific finding] → [Additional study evidence, clustered or individualized] → [Return to / expansion of thematic claim]

**Example from Gilmore (2025):**
> *[Claim]* Sustained, dialogic learning communities help novices interpret the expectations of the role and view research as integral rather than oppositional to teaching.
>
> *[Named study + method + finding]* In a year-long professional development program for 11 teacher educators in Israel, Shagrir (2010) found that combining theoretical reading, practice-based discussion, and peer feedback enabled participants to articulate "the uniqueness of all aspects of the teacher education profession." Meeting outside their home institutions legitimized time spent reading and writing as part of their teaching work.
>
> *[Additional evidence, clustered]* Similar affordances appeared in faculty and doctoral writing groups (Murphy et al., 2014; Roberts & Weston, 2014), where collective reflection on drafts and publication pressures helped participants connect scholarship and pedagogy.
>
> *[Return/expansion]* Through these dialogic structures, novices came to see writing and research as ways of theorizing and improving practice rather than as competing demands.

**Key features of this structure:**
- The claim opens; the evidence follows; the claim returns expanded — not restated
- At least one named study appears in the evidence layer with specific methodological and finding detail
- Clustered citations appear only *after* a named study has established the pattern — they amplify, they don't substitute
- The return is a synthesis, not a summary

## Clustering vs. Individualizing

The decision about when to name a study specifically vs. when to cluster it with others is one of the primary analytical decisions in granular synthesis.

**Individualize (name the study with method and finding) when:**
- The study is the primary or strongest evidence for the claim
- The sample size, design, or context makes it methodologically notable or distinctive
- The finding is counterintuitive, contested, or more specific than a general claim would convey
- The study's geographic or institutional context matters to the argument
- You are comparing two specific studies against each other
- No other named study has yet established the pattern this claim requires

**Cluster (parenthetical citations) when:**
- Multiple studies converge on the same point and a named study has already established the pattern
- You are listing studies that share a methodological characteristic ("most of these are self-studies")
- The studies amplify or confirm a claim rather than establish it
- The point is so well-established in the field that the citation is standard attribution rather than evidentiary

**Wrong:**
> "Research has consistently found that teachers inherit curricular defaults rather than selecting from scratch (Applebee, 1993; Zancanella, 1991; Soter, 1992; Freedman et al., 1999; Obidah & Howard, 2005)." — Convergence claimed, not demonstrated.

**Right** (note the sentence openings vary; see Author-Led Paragraphs below):
> "Applebee's (1993) national survey of 322 secondary English teachers — the largest such study to date — found that canonical texts dominated the high school curriculum, with Shakespeare, Steinbeck, and the same handful of titles appearing across schools regardless of region or demographics. Zancanella's (1991) smaller, intensive study of eleven teachers in a single district corroborated this pattern at the classroom level, showing that teachers described their lists as inherited rather than chosen — 'what was there when I arrived.' Later qualitative studies (Soter, 1992; Freedman et al., 1999) documented the same inheritance logic across different geographic and institutional contexts."

## Author-Led Paragraphs

Study anchoring can tip into a paragraph where every sentence opens with the author: "Smith (2019) found... Smith also notes... She argues... Jones (2020) extends..." Austin flagged this pattern; his drafts show it at 27 times the rate of the published work he reads. Lead some sentences with the finding, the setting, or the claim, and move the citation inside or to the end:

> *Author-led:* Applebee (1993) surveyed 650 schools. Applebee found that 94% of teachers valued their freedom of approach. He also found that only 30% reported complete freedom of choice.
>
> *Varied (from a paragraph Dorothea praised):* In the public-school sample, 94% of teachers identified the freedom to develop their own instructional style and approach as a strength of their English programs. Asked separately about text selection, however, only 30% reported complete freedom of choice, while 5% reported little or no leeway (Applebee, 1993, pp. 28, 77-78).

## Whose Claim Is It?

"Is this Rios' conclusions or yours? That has to be made clear in a review of the lit." Every evaluative sentence must show whether it reports the author's conclusion or Austin's reading. Quote the author's own key terms. Never adjudicate silently ("Kolbas is right that..."); say "Kolbas argues..., and the survey evidence supports this because...". Check every cross-study claim against the studies: "Do the studies show that?" / "Is that the case across all of the studies or just these two?"

## Participant Voice as Evidence

Participant quotes from primary studies should function as concrete instantiation of findings — not as illustration, not as rhetorical ornament, not as a substitute for describing what the study found. They must always be anchored to a specific study and appear *after* the methodological context has been established.

**Rules:**
- Keep quotes brief: under 20 words when possible
- Always attribute to the specific study with page number: "(Murray et al., 2011, p. 265)"
- The quote must follow from an already-described finding — it illustrates, it does not establish
- Never let a participant quote stand in for a study's findings

**Good (from Gilmore, 2025):**
> "One English teacher educator, nine years into higher education, continued to identify exclusively with teaching: 'I think "lecturer" is almost like a term of abuse. I don't want to be one of you, thank you very much' (Murray et al., 2011, p. 265)."

Note what precedes this in Gilmore: the study is named, the participant type is established, the finding about identity resistance is described — and then the quote arrives as a vivid instance of that finding.

**Bad:**
> "As one teacher educator explained, 'I don't want to be one of you, thank you very much.'" — No attribution, no methodological anchoring, no connection to a specific finding.

**Rule:** A participant quote is not a study. It is a data point within a study. Treat it accordingly.

## Naming Gaps and Limits

Every thematic synthesis must identify not only what studies found but what they *did not* investigate, could not establish, or systematically excluded. This is not hedging — it is part of the empirical record, and it is where the dissertation's own argument can enter.

**Pattern:**
> [What the studies found] → [What the studies failed to address or could not establish] → [What that gap means for the argument being built]

**Example from Gilmore (2025):**
> "Across the reviewed literature, studies rarely investigate systematic approaches to preparation, or the concrete knowledge and practices of the teacher educator as their central focus. Furthermore, no study provided evidence about the effects of preparation on teacher education practices or outcomes — whether or how particular kinds of preparation make a difference."

**In Austin's dissertation context:** Naming a gap in one strand's empirical record often names the problem that the next strand addresses, or the limit that the theoretical framework is required to explain. A study that documents *what* teachers selected but not *why* is not merely incomplete — it is incomplete in a theoretically meaningful way.

---

## Cross-Study Comparison

When two or more studies can be set directly against each other — different methods, different samples, different findings — the comparison itself is evidence. Use it.

**Pattern:**
> "[Study A], by contrast with [Study B], found..." or "[Study A]'s finding that X is complicated by [Study B]'s finding that Y, conducted across a larger and more diverse sample."

This move establishes that the field has internal tensions, not just a settled consensus. It also prepares the reader for the synthetic claim that will resolve (or hold open) the tension.

## The Pivot into Theory

After empirical grounding and thematic synthesis have been completed, the theoretical move arrives. The pivot follows `/academic-writing`'s **Theoretical Pivot**: theory is the explanation demanded by the evidence, not a framework imported from outside.

**Pivot signals.** Mark the move plainly and keep the agents human. Prefer:
- "Taken together, these studies show [specific finding]. [Framework] helps explain why: ..."
- "The studies describe [pattern] but rarely explain [gap]. I use [concept] to name ..." (Austin's praised move: "I use defensibility to name this emergent advantage.")
- "[Theoretical figure]'s concept of X describes what these studies document without naming."

Avoid "What this pattern reveals...", "The convergence ... is not incidental — it is the shape of...", and "The structural consistency here...". They let an abstraction act as the agent and read as the narrator verdicts Austin flagged in his own slop.

**Inviolable rule:** Do not pivot to theory before the empirical pattern has been named with specific studies. If you have not yet said what the studies found — with named studies, specific methods, and specific findings — you are not ready to theorize.

## Applying This Skill in the Dissertation Context

Austin's literature review is organized around throughlines — each throughline is a thematic argument built from a body of empirical studies. The throughline structure presupposes this skill: you cannot argue that text selection is an *inherited* curriculum practice if you haven't first shown, with specific studies, specific samples, and specific findings, that this is what the empirical record documents.

**Per-study revision workflow (Dorothea's feedback):**
1. Open a section draft.
2. For each named study, ask: Does the reader know (a) who conducted it, (b) on whom, (c) where, (d) using what method, and (e) what it specifically found? If any of these are missing, add them to the attribution.
3. For each parenthetical cluster, ask: Is there at least one named study that has established the pattern this cluster amplifies? If not, promote one study from the cluster to a named attribution.
4. For each instance of "studies show" or "researchers have found," ask: Which studies? What did they find, specifically? Add the specificity.
5. Check the order: is the foundational study discussed fully first, then earlier and later studies framed against it? Does a transition announce each move between kinds of studies?
6. Check attribution and descriptors: does every name have a descriptor, and is every evaluative sentence marked as the author's or Austin's?
7. Check signposting: are the key findings stated at the opening and labeled in a summary?
8. Only after steps 1–7 are complete: check whether the theoretical claim at the end of the section is supported by what the evidence section has now established.
9. Run `python3 style-lab/scripts/check_draft.py DRAFT.docx` when available. Its report lists Dorothea's earlier comments on paragraphs that match ones she has already read, so unresolved comments surface before she sees them again.

## Common Failures (What Dorothea's Critique Catches)

1. **Pre-emptive synthesis**: Introducing the theoretical argument before showing the evidence for it. The argument arrives before the empirical record has been established.

2. **Citation substitution**: Stacking parenthetical citations as if that constitutes synthesis. `(Applebee, 1993; Zancanella, 1991; Soter, 1992)` is not evidence — it is a pointer to evidence that still needs to be presented.

3. **Generic invocation**: "The literature shows" / "Scholars argue" / "Research has demonstrated" — these phrases invoke a body of evidence without specifying it.

4. **Finding-free attribution**: "Zancanella (1991) studied text selection" — names the study, conveys nothing about what it found.

5. **Detached participant quotes**: Embedding participant quotes without methodological anchoring — the reader doesn't know who said it, in what study, among what participants, under what conditions.

6. **Theorizing before cataloguing gaps**: Claiming the literature reveals a structural problem before specifying what the literature actually contains and what it is missing. The gap is part of the evidence.

7. **Misplaced synthesis**: Describing what studies are *about* (topic) rather than what they *found* (finding). "Several studies have examined the role of the anthology in text selection" is topic description, not synthesis.

8. **Hidden chronology**: Presenting later studies before earlier ones without saying so, or describing a debate's public reception before the debate itself.

9. **Unlabeled findings**: Key findings implied rather than stated, labeled, and repeated.

10. **Vague names**: A study, commentary, or person without a descriptor; an analytic term that drifts ("field," "curriculum," "account").

## Interaction with `/academic-writing`

These two skills operate at different registers and in sequence, not in competition.

| Task | Skill |
|---|---|
| Naming a study with its method, sample, and finding | `/critical-synthesis` |
| Clustering studies that converge on a pattern | `/critical-synthesis` |
| Ordering studies, transitions, roadmaps, labeled summaries | `/critical-synthesis` |
| Naming gaps in the empirical record | `/critical-synthesis` |
| Building the specificity sandwich | `/critical-synthesis` |
| Pivoting from evidence to theoretical claim | Both, in transition |
| Genealogical opening (concrete historical event) | `/academic-writing` |
| Coining or deploying a theoretical concept | `/academic-writing` |
| The Structural Continuity Move | `/academic-writing` |
| Productive aporia / holding tension open | `/academic-writing` |

In practice, most literature review body sections alternate registers: granular study introduction (`/critical-synthesis`) → thematic synthesis (`/critical-synthesis`) → theoretical argument (`/academic-writing`). The `/academic-writing` moves earn their authority precisely because `/critical-synthesis` has done its work first.
